STOCK GAME 
MAKING WIREFRAMES MORE ROBUST FOR MOBILE APP DEVELOPMENT: A CASE STUDY

My Role: Product Designer 

Timeline: 2 Weeks

Deliverables: 3 Key Use Cases, User stories, Clickable Prototype, Wireframes

Product Type: Mobile App

This case study is about the importance of iteration on both the product process and the design/development process. 

My company (a small design agency) is growing. As we scale we need to have more robust wireframes to delivery to developers. After doing a review of recent wireframes I created for a stock market game app, we came up with a plan to make sure developers have more complete spec and wireframes with alt states, error states, and empty states. 

Intro: From Zero to One - The Initial Product Design Process 

 At my company, we release app wireframes and user stories to developers in distinct phases, often beginning with onboarding. Our initial product design often looks like this: 

1. Kickoff Call with Client 

2. Conduct any Discovery Research and planning. This usually include 2-3 of the following deliverables

    Competitive Research 

    Market Research 

    Targeting Documents 

    Surveys 

    Discovery Interviews 

    Personas 

    Value Proposition & Key Features 

    Journey Maps (useful if project is a redesign)

3. Draft 3 Key Use Cases based on client needs & research insights

 Get client approval on Use Cases 

4. Draft Interaction Notes and Wireframes for Clickable Prototype

Get Client approval of Interaction Notes and Wireframes

Create Clickable Prototype, complete with initial visual design 

Get client Approval of Clickable Prototype

5. Test Clickable Prototype with 5-7 Users 

Make any changes as needed & present insights to client 

6. Create User Stories

Iterate on User Stories with team/developer feedback

7. Create Wireframes based on User Stories

Wireframe versions are then subdivided into smaller features/or sections (A, B, etc.) 

We often begin with Onboarding, which we would name “A.”

We get client approval for each smaller part of a version, make any needed iterations, and then give the wireframes and user stories to the development team 

2. Get client approval for first feature (For example, ”A:Onboarding”)

3. Make changes as needed

4. Deliver first feature to developer

5. Work on the next feature/section (In this case it was “B: News & Stocks”) 

Crafting Use Cases 

For this project (building a stock market game) our client had already conducted much of the early research.  For that reason, we began our design process by drafting 3 Key Use Cases, which are similar to User Flows. I helped craft these, and then my teammate and I created and edited a clickable prototype. 

Creating and Testing the Clickable Prototype

Then I tested the clickable prototype with 5 users and presented the results to our client. A key takeaway from our usability testing was that users were unsure of how basic tenets of the game worked by the time they were asked to pay for the app. Based on current industry trends, I suggested adding onboarding pages to our log-in that would help users gain a basic understanding of gameplay. This was then incorporated into our user stories and wireframes. 

Drafting User Stories & Initial Wireframes

After we finished our usability testing, I drafted over 200 user stories for our MVP. After I got approval for the user stories and made changes based on development requirements, I began drafting the wireframes for our first two features, A:Onboarding and B:Stock and News. I presented the onboarding wireframes to our client and my managers, and they were approved by the client. 

Design Session & Review (with Developers and Designers) 

Then, we held a cross-team design and wireframe review session with our technical lead, design lead, and a QA tester. The goal was to review the initial wireframes, iterate on them, and brainstorm changes we could make to improve our overall process. 

Why do a Design Review? 

We have struggled with developers creating solutions that don’t match the wireframes in the past, costing us thousands of dollars and weeks of development time. One challenge is that much of our development team is overseas, and work is being done while our management team is not working. Our wireframes are a “source of truth” for a project, and need to be so easily understood that they can manage developers while we are asleep. 

Iteration

Based on that design session, I made some changes to my wireframes for the first two features (Onboarding and News and Stocks) adding notes and alternate states to help guide developers and not leave any room for costly confusion.

The Wireframes! 

Below you can see examples of alternate states I added to one “feature” of the wireframes (Section B: News & Stocks) which is the stock news and watchlist section of our game app. The “Main State” wireframes are the original wireframes that I created (initially they had a few alt. states and explanations laid out on sticky notes). The wireframes labeled “Alt. State” are wireframes I added after our design session. Instead of only using interaction notes, gesture notes, or partial alt states, we decided that each alt state needs an entire wireframe.
