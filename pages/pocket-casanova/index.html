Research for a Mobile Date-Tracking App: A Case Study

My Role
UX Researcher
Length
2 Months

What do you do if your client's mobile app idea just isn't that great? You do research to validate their assumptions and help them build the best possible product, of course!

This case study demonstrates the benefit of research and shows how my design agency and I saved a client tens of thousands of dollars by carefully testing and validating assumptions. Ultimately, my team was able to help our client pivot to an idea that better matched our core users' needs.

Deliverables

My Research Deliverables were:

  • 10 Discovery Interviews
  • A 1,000-person quantitative survey
  • 2 Market Analyses (before and after a pivot)
  • 2 Competitive Analyses (before and after a pivot)

I also delivered Strategy Documents, including:

  • Key Features
  • Problem Statements
  • Market Segmentation & Targeting
  • Personas

Summary

Where we started

Our client's original idea was an app that would be based on a baseball theme, and would turn dating into a social and "gamified" experience. They wanted an app that would be a digital "little black book," and give their users a way to track and rate dates. They wanted to target younger men who (they believed) want to share information about their dates (including ratings, etc), with friends. They also wanted an element of gamification – users would be rewarded for how many dates they went on and other metrics (which they called 'hits') and compared with their friends on the app.

Where we ended up

…After doing qualitative research, a market analysis, and competitive research, we were able to pitch a pivot to the client. Now we are working on an app that was more of a dating companion, where users of any gender could get advice on dating questions from peers and professionals.

My Research Process

Identifying and Validating Assumptions

First, my team wanted to know if our client's assumptions about their target audience were correct. We began our process by identifying assumptions that we could test.

Our clients initial assumptions included:

  • Young men want to share detailed information about their sexual and dating encounters with friends
  • Young men want to get feedback from friends on dates
  • Men are interested in scoring and being compared with each other based on aspects of their dating lives (such as how many dates they go on in a week)

Interviews

Along with the Market and Competitive Analyses, I conducted 10 discovery interviews, with 5 men and 5 women. In my 10 initial interviews I made discoveries that seemed to be at odds with our client's assumptions.

Interview Insights: I discovered that:

  • None of the men we interviewed (5 total men) were interested in sharing details with friends
  • Women shared more details with friends more often
  • Women were more likely to share very intimate details with friends

I presented the interview results to the client. However, because our interviews included only 5 men, and only some of our users were in the 18-29 range, our client was not convinced that we had adequately targeted their ideal user. So to examine some of the questions raised by our interviews further, we proposed a survey of a larger number of users. These would help us validate if the insights and conclusions from our interviews were correct.

Survey

I drafted questions for a survey to gauge interest in the proposed app features and better understand our users.

I surveyed 1,000 18-25 year olds using Survey Monkey and presented the results and my analysis the client. In the Survey Analysis below, you can view the questions (which I drafted and then workshopped with my team) as well as our insights and takeaways.

Survey Analysis

Survey Takeaway: One of the main insights that would inform how we moved forward was that: although we didn't realize that getting advice from friends and peers on dating was important, it actually turned out to be one of the most highly-rated features in our survey.

Pivot

After analyzing the results, our client asked us to pivot and do new Market Research and Competitive Research documents based on a slightly different idea: they wanted to know if there was a market of an app that didn't have as much of an emphasis on gamification and sharing dating details with friends. Instead, they wanted to know if there was a market for an app that would emphasize getting feedback from friends and peers on dating and compatibility. They wanted to know a) if there is demand and b) is the demand already being met by other apps or software?

I did another round of research and revised our Market Research document and Competitive Research document. You can view the revised versions with the updates below.

Competitive Research V2

Pivot Takeaways: We did learn that there is a market for getting advice from friends and peers, and that our client's key features are not being met directly by other apps. Another interesting insight we found was that many men actually valued getting anonymous feedback from forum-like environments. Many men preferred getting anonymous advice over getting advice from people they know personally.

Personas & Targeting documents

Based on what we found from our second round of market and competitive research, we pitched a new idea to the client.

Our new idea was an app that would be more like a "dating companion." The app would allow daters to get advice both from friends and anonymously. Users would be able to post specific questions in a forum-like setting and also message questions to their friends. Other users, including both counseling professionals and regular users who simply enjoy giving advice, would be able to give feedback to those questions. An element of gamification would be added by giving users who answer questions points. We expect that future versions of the app will even allow counselors and "armchair dating-experts" to earn money by giving advice and counseling services.

Next Steps

The client liked the idea, and now the app has entered the design and development phase. We were able to validate some assumptions and correct others, and ultimately give the client a better-formed idea of who their potential users are, what their users' needs are, and how best they can meet them.
